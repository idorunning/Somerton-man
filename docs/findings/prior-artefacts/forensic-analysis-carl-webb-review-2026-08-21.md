# Review - unattributed Carl Webb inscription analysis

**Date:** 21 August 2026

**Verdict: Retain as a hypothesis-generating artefact, not as a forensic finding. The report
does not establish that Carl Webb wrote the inscription or that the letters encode poetry. It
contains a wrong birth date, overstates the official status of the Webb identification, gives
invalid archival references for electoral rolls, misidentifies the contemporary Australian
engineering-drawing standard, and does not provide the evidence or method needed to reproduce
its handwriting and statistical conclusions.**

## Writing and presentation critique

The original is visually polished and easy to scan, but its writing creates an authority that
the underlying material does not earn.

- **The title overclaims the method.** "Forensic epigraphy" and "historical biomechanical
  assessment" suggest a recognised examination with validated procedures. What follows is an
  occupationally informed interpretation of a reproduction.
- **Observation and conclusion are fused.** A visible block capital becomes a claimed stroke
  order, then an engineering habit, then evidence for Webb. Each step needs separate evidence
  and an alternative explanation.
- **Precision substitutes for documentation.** "Within 0.1 mm tolerance" sounds decisive but
  no scale, coordinates, uncertainty, repeat measurement or image is supplied.
- **Technical vocabulary is sometimes misused.** Initials cannot establish phonological flow,
  morphology or iambic metre without the words. The proposed device is an initialism or mnemonic,
  not necessarily an acrostic. `x-height` is also the wrong measure for all-capital lettering;
  `cap height` is the relevant term.
- **Citations are decorative rather than auditable.** Several are incomplete, absent from the
  bibliography or attached to the wrong archive series. The prose nevertheless uses
  "confirms", "determined" and "demonstrate".
- **The design hides the evidential gap.** The key figure is typeset text rather than the source
  inscription, while the references occupy a mostly empty third page. A credible report would
  use that space for an annotated plate, measurements, competing readings and limitations.

The corrected report at
`docs/reports/carl-webb-inscription-critical-assessment-2026-08-21.md` keeps the useful research
questions but changes the genre from purported forensic finding to critical research note.

## What the report proposes

The report advances three connected propositions:

1. the block capitals show motor habits acquired through Webb's work as an instrument maker;
2. particular `G`, `S`, `C` and overmarked-`O` forms are associated with engineering,
   telegraphy or editorial practice; and
3. the letter sequence is probably mnemonic initials for unpublished English quatrains rather
   than a cipher.

These are legitimate ideas to test. The problem is that the document presents them as findings
without supplying the comparison material, source images, validation or statistical results
needed to test them.

## Forensic adequacy

The label "forensic report" is not evidence of a forensic process. As a current benchmark, the
Forensic Science Regulator requires methods to be fit for purpose and validated, data sources
and assumptions to be clear, practitioners to be demonstrably competent, and evaluative work
to be peer reviewed. This document supplies none of those controls. The benchmark is not being
applied retrospectively as law governing an Australian historical document; it is used only to
show why the report's categorical language exceeds the material it presents.

The analysis also reasons from occupation to authorship. Even if engineering training explains
some lettering conventions, many people could share those conventions. A useful authorship
comparison would require sufficient, authenticated and approximately contemporaneous Webb
writing, assessed against the questioned marks and relevant alternative writers. No such known
material appears in the PDF.

## What can be carried forward

- Webb's documented occupation makes technical-lettering training a reasonable research lead.
- Whether the sequence is an initialism remains a viable competing explanation.
- A terminal `C`/incomplete-`S` sensitivity analysis is worth running if the primary image can
  support the distinction.
- Historical engineering and telegraphy manuals may supply reference alphabets, but they need
  to be located and compared before any claimed correlation is accepted.

## What must not be carried forward as fact

- that Webb authored the inscription;
- that the report measured a 0.1 mm glyph match;
- that `G` stroke order or an interrupted `S` has been established;
- that Webb was a telegraphist or used a telegraphic transcription convention;
- that the terminal character is `S`;
- that the inscription is a poetic acrostic;
- that its initials establish FitzGerald's metre; or
- that named agencies ruled out cipher explanations in the terms claimed.

## Reproducible next tests

1. Obtain the highest-generation available police image and record its custody, processing,
   scale and compression history.
2. Locate authenticated Webb handwriting from a comparable period and record provenance for
   each specimen.
3. Transcribe all disputed characters as explicit variants before analysis.
4. Have a competent document examiner define features and comparison criteria before seeing
   Webb's occupation or the proposed conclusion, reducing contextual bias.
5. Locate the 1941 and 1946 `CZ1` drawing standards and any relevant PMG training manuals;
   compare their actual alphabets rather than occupational labels.
6. Re-run the initialism claim with a published corpus, null model, code, uncertainty and
   controls for transcription variants and researcher degrees of freedom.
7. Identify the report's author and request the source plates, measurements, statistical output
   and the full `Clegg, 1939`, Defence, FBI and `Abbott et al., 2019` references.

## Caveats

This is a source and methods audit, not a fresh expert handwriting examination. It did not
inspect the original Rubáiyát, a first-generation police plate or known Webb handwriting. The
absence of a traceable citation in this review does not prove that the underlying source never
existed; it means the PDF does not identify it well enough to verify. The repository's own
standard transcription is also provisional and must remain open to primary-source correction.
