# Carl Webb and the Somerton Man inscription

## A critical assessment of the technical-lettering hypothesis

**Document status:** Corrected research note - not a forensic authorship report
**Date:** 21 August 2026
**Version:** 2.0
**Prepared for:** Somerton Man itinerary-hypothesis research repository

## Scope

This note revises an unattributed three-page document supplied to the project under the title
*Paleographic & Motor-Habit Analysis of the Somerton Man Inscription*. The original proposed
that Carl Webb wrote the inscription, that his instrument-making work explained particular
letterforms, and that the letters were mnemonic initials for unpublished poetry.

Those are hypotheses, not established findings. The supplied report contains useful questions,
but its factual errors, missing source material and unsupported precision prevent it from being
treated as forensic evidence. This revision separates observation from inference, corrects the
traceable errors and defines the evidence needed for a proper test.

## Executive assessment

**The surviving material does not presently support an attribution of the inscription to Carl
Webb.** Webb's reported occupation as an instrument maker makes technical-lettering training a
reasonable lead, but occupation is not handwriting identification. No authenticated Webb
handwriting has been compared with the questioned inscription, and the supplied report contains
no original inscription image, calibration, measurements or documented examination method.

The proposition that the letters are word initials remains plausible in a broad sense. The
evidence does not distinguish unpublished poetry from a personal mnemonic, an itinerary, a
cipher, notes copied from another source or meaningless writing. Initial letters cannot, by
themselves, establish poetic metre.

## Corrected factual basis

### Identity and biographical record

The supplied report gives Webb's birth as 1 May 1905. The birth-certificate information
reported by ABC gives **16 November 1905** in Footscray, Victoria. The supplied report also
describes the Webb identification as confirmed by the University of Adelaide and Forensic
Science South Australia. That is too strong. Abbott and Fitzpatrick announced the result in
2022. On 6 July 2026, SAPOL said its coronial investigation was still under way and its report
would be provided to the Coroner when complete.

This note therefore uses **Carl Webb as the leading working identification**, while keeping the
distinction between a research identification and a published coronial finding.

The report's references to National Archives of Australia series A433 and B2455 as Victorian
electoral-roll sources are wrong. A433 is restricted-immigration correspondence and B2455
contains First Australian Imperial Force personnel dossiers. The occupation of instrument
maker is supported instead by the 1941 marriage certificate described in the ABC biographical
account. The broader labels "electrical engineer", "poet" and "lyricist" require their own
identified primary records before being used as established biography.

### The Rubaiyat and inscription

State Records of South Australia records that police received the book on 22 July 1949. It had
reportedly been found in a car around 30 November 1948 and contained a handwritten note thought
to be a code and a telephone number. The supplied report's more specific description of a 1941
Whitcombe & Tombs edition, rear flyleaf, graphite indentation, oblique tungsten lighting and
ultraviolet-induced fluorescence is not supported by a cited police exhibit or technical
report within the document. Those details should remain provisional until the relevant primary
record is produced.

### Working transcription

The following is the repository's current working transcription, not a facsimile:

```text
Line 1   W R G O A B A B D
Line 2   M L I A O I
Line 3   W T B I M P A N E T P
Line 4   M L I A B O A I A Q C   [commonly reported as crossed out]
Line 5   I T T M T S A M S T G A B
```

The supplied report instead uses `M` at the start of line 3, treats line 2 as deleted and
reinterprets the terminal `C` on line 4 as an incomplete `S`. It does not explain why those
variants were selected. Future analysis must preserve all plausible variants and state in
advance which version is being tested.

## Assessment of the technical-lettering hypothesis

### What can be observed

Available reproductions show upright block capitals with broadly uniform stroke weight. That
description is an observation. It is reasonable to ask whether technical work, schooling,
military service or routine clerical practice influenced the style.

The supplied report moves directly from that general appearance to Webb's occupation. That is
an authorship inference, and it needs comparison evidence. Many tradespeople and non-tradespeople
were taught block capitals. A shared convention can reduce the individuality of writing rather
than identify a writer.

### Why occupation does not identify the writer

A defensible comparison would require:

- authenticated Webb handwriting from a similar period;
- enough natural writing to show variation within Webb's own hand;
- contemporaneous comparison writing from other plausible writers;
- a high-generation, documented image of the questioned inscription;
- declared criteria for inclusion, exclusion and uncertainty; and
- an examiner who records questioned features before learning the proposed identity and
  occupational theory.

None of that material appears in the supplied report. It therefore cannot support a conclusion
about authorship.

### The historical drawing-standard claim

The report cites an "ASA CB1" standard from 1934, revised in 1944. The National Library of
Australia catalogues the relevant national works as *Australian standard engineering drawing
practice*, numbers **CZ.1-1941** and **CZ1-1946**. The report's identifier, title and dates were
not reconciled with those records.

Even the correct standard would show only that a lettering convention existed. It would not
show that Webb was trained under it, retained its letterforms, wrote the inscription or used a
particular stroke order. The actual standards and Webb's training record must be examined
before the hypothesis can move beyond background plausibility.

## Assessment of individual-character claims

### Letter `G`

The report says both `G` characters were started at the internal spur and written clockwise.
It gives no annotated plate or junction analysis demonstrating that sequence. Stroke order may
sometimes be inferred from overlap, interruption, pressure or deposition evidence, but a static
low-generation reproduction does not automatically preserve those features. No stroke-order
finding is accepted here.

### Terminal `C` or incomplete `S`

The report claims that the terminal line-4 character matches the upper lobe of line-5 `S`
characters to within 0.1 mm. It provides no image scale, coordinates, uncertainty, repeat
measurements or source resolution. The precision is therefore unsupported.

The further claim that "phonological and morphological flow" supports `S` is misplaced. The
underlying words are unknown, so their sound and structure cannot be examined. The character
must remain a declared `C`/`S` variant until better source material supports discrimination.

### Marks around `O`

The overmark may be a deletion, correction, separator, incidental contact or another notation.
Calling it a proofreader's null sign or a laboratory-log convention without a matched reference
alphabet is speculation. It should be recorded descriptively before an interpretation is
assigned.

### Telegraphic `S`

The supplied report cites "Clegg, 1939" for a top bar used to distinguish `S` from `8` or `5`,
but gives no title, publisher, page or bibliographic entry. The claim has been removed pending a
traceable PMG training manual or other period source. It would still require evidence that Webb
received or used that training.

## Initialism, cipher and poetry

The sequence is short and transcription-dependent. That limits the power of frequency,
periodicity and language tests. The supplied report says Australian Defence, the FBI and modern
computational linguists ruled out relevant cipher models, but it does not identify the reports
or reproduce the tests. It also cites "Abbott et al., 2019" in the text without including that
work in its references.

A University of Adelaide project report from 2010 reached a more cautious result. Its
text-type testing found no significant differences between the categories examined and said
the significance of observed initialisms was undetermined. That does not prove a cipher, an
initialism or any particular source text.

The supplied report's phrase "poetic acrostic" is also imprecise. If each letter represents the
first letter of a word, the result would be an initial-letter mnemonic or initialism. An
acrostic ordinarily uses selected letters from a completed text to form another message. More
importantly, metre depends on words, syllables and stress. A string of initials cannot establish
iambic tetrameter or pentameter without recovering the words.

At least four broad models remain live:

1. initials of remembered or drafted text;
2. a cipher or encoding scheme that cannot be resolved from the available length;
3. initials of places, movements or another personal list; and
4. notation whose intended meaning is now unrecoverable, including meaningless writing.

The present evidence does not rank unpublished poetry above those alternatives.

## A reproducible way forward

1. **Secure the source image.** Identify the highest-generation surviving image, its holder,
   capture process, scale, alterations and chain of copies.
2. **Freeze transcription variants.** Have independent readers transcribe the image before
   being shown proposed solutions. Publish every disputed character and deletion rule.
3. **Locate known Webb writing.** Seek authenticated, approximately contemporaneous exemplars
   with enough material to characterise normal variation.
4. **Separate observation from context.** A competent document examiner should record features
   before receiving Webb's identity, occupation or the preferred explanation.
5. **Test occupational references.** Examine the actual CZ1 standards, relevant trade-school
   materials and PMG manuals. Compare their illustrated alphabets rather than relying on job
   titles.
6. **Pre-register computational tests.** State the transcription, corpus, null model, scoring
   rule and decision threshold before running the real sequence. Publish code and full output.
7. **Use discriminating alternatives.** Compare poetry, prose, personal mnemonics, place
   sequences and random controls. A positive result against a weak baseline is not evidence for
   one preferred story.

## Conclusion

The original document sounds more certain than its evidence permits. Its strongest contribution
is not a solved authorship or poetic interpretation, but a set of questions that can be tested:
whether the lettering resembles period technical instruction, whether disputed characters can
be resolved from better imagery, and whether an initial-letter model performs better than fair
alternatives.

For now, the technically defensible conclusion is narrow. Webb may have had occupational
experience relevant to block lettering. That fact does not identify his hand, recover the
missing words or determine the purpose of the inscription.
